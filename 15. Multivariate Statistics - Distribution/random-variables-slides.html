Mapping Reality to Math

We already know how to describe data once we have it — mean, median. But new data hasn't happened yet. How do we deal with not knowing, ahead of time?

1.1 — The Random Variable

Not solve-for-x. A translator.

A random variable isn't algebra's x — there's nothing to solve for. It's a rule that turns something that just happens into a number you can count.

Reach into a jar of candies without looking. Did you grab your favorite flavor? That's not a number — it's luck. The rule X turns it into one: X(favorite) = 1, X(not favorite) = 0.

The luck is in which candy your hand finds. The rule itself never changes — it always turns "favorite" into 1.

Try it: reach into the jar a few times. Each reach is a surprise — but the row of 1s and 0s it leaves behind is exactly what we do math with.

1.2 — From One to Many
↳ One reach gave us one number. What happens once we do it a thousand times?

One try is a number. Lots of tries make a shape.

Every kid who grabs a handful from the candy jar produces one number — say X = candies in the handful. One kid, one number: 2, or 0, or 5.

Now do that with a thousand handfuls. The exact numbers stop being the point — what shows up is how often each amount happens. That shape is called a distribution.

That's the big idea: a random variable is the rule. A distribution is what the rule looks like after it happens a thousand times.

1.3 — Discrete vs. Continuous
↳ Since a random variable makes numbers — what kind of numbers can they be?

Things you count vs. things you measure.

Discrete means you count it, one at a time. Candies in your hand: 0, 1, 2, 3 … There's no such thing as two and a half candies.

Continuous means you measure it, and it can be any amount in between. How tall you are. Between 100 cm and 100.1 cm tall, there are endless heights in between.

1.4 — PMF vs. PDF
↳ Now that we've measured both kinds — how do we read the chance of each value?

Bars you can read. Curves you must measure.

PMF — for candies, something you count. It's the same bar chart from before. The bar's height is the chance: P(candies = 2) — just read the bar.

PDF — for height, something you measure. P(height = 110.000…cm) is exactly zero — there are endless possible heights, so no single one gets any of the chance. We can only ask about a range: the area under the curve between two heights.

The Shape of the Universe

We've watched randomness turn into a shape. Now: is every shape really about chance? And once two things are shaped differently, how do we ever compare them fairly?

2.1 — Two Kinds of Shapes

Is it a fact, or a guess?

Look in a cookie jar with your eyes open. You can just count what's there: 12 chocolate chip, 8 oatmeal. That's a fact — nobody's guessing anything.

Now close your eyes and reach in. You don't know which one you'll pull out — but you can still say the chance: 12 out of 20 are chocolate chip, so there's a 60% chance.

Non-probabilistic = eyes open, just counting what's real. Probabilistic = eyes closed, guessing the odds of what comes next.

Flip the switch below. Same jar, same cookies — only whether your eyes are open changes what the numbers mean.

2.2 — What Actually Changes
↳ So what's the actual rule that turns a head-count into a chance?

Everything has to add up to 100%.

The fact-count in the jar can be anything at all — 3 cookies, 20, a thousand. There's no rule about what the total has to be.

But the chance of grabbing chocolate chip plus the chance of grabbing oatmeal must always add up to exactly 100%. There's no wiggle room — you're guaranteed to pull out one or the other.

The trick for turning counts into chances: divide each flavor's count by the total. That's the entire recipe.

2.3 — The Normal (Gaussian) Distribution

Why the same bump keeps showing up.

Heights, how long it takes to tie your shoes, how many candies fit in a handful — totally different things, yet they all end up making the exact same bump shape.

Here's why: imagine flipping a coin 8 times. Move one step right for heads, one step left for tails. Most of the time you end up somewhere near the middle — there just aren't many ways to get 8 heads in a row, but tons of ways to get about half and half.

2.4 — Big Compared to What?
↳ A mouse and a dog can't be measured on the same ruler. So what do we do instead?

A giant mouse vs. a big dog — who's more unusual?

A mouse is usually about 10 cm long. A dog is usually about 60 cm long. You can't compare those sizes directly — they're not even the same kind of thing.

So don't ask "how big are they." Ask "how big are they for their own kind?" A mouse that's way bigger than other mice is unusual — even if a totally average dog is still bigger than it in real life.

That's what normalizing means: instead of comparing raw sizes, compare how far each one sits from what's typical for its own group.

2.5 — You Already Do This: Sharing Pizza
↳ This is the exact same trick as normalizing — just with dinner.

2 slices of 4, or 4 slices of 8?

You eat 2 slices of a pizza that's cut into 4. Your friend eats 4 slices of a pizza cut into 8. Who ate more pizza?

You both ate exactly half: 2/4 = 50%, and 4/8 = 50%. Different slice counts, same fraction of the whole pizza.

Turning something into a percentage is normalizing it — rescaling it to the same 0-to-100 scale, no matter how many pieces you started with.

2.6 — Same Curve, New Ruler
↳ Here's the actual formula behind everything we've been doing by hand.

z = (x − μ) ÷ σ

Here's a curve of real values — height, in centimeters. It has its own average (μ) and its own spread (σ).

Normalizing means relabeling every point on the x-axis with one formula: z = (x − μ) ÷ σ. Subtract the average, then divide by the spread.

Watch what happens when you flip the switch: the bump doesn't move, doesn't stretch, doesn't change shape at all. Only the ruler underneath it changes — from raw centimeters to "how many spreads from average."

The Universal Translator & the Ultimate Law

We can now put any two bell curves on one shared ruler. So: how do we compare two completely different ones — and why does averaging always, eventually, save us?

3.1 — Apples to Oranges

A 90 on a math test, or a 1200 on the SAT?

A 90/100 on a math test (class average 85, spread 5) and a 1200/1600 on the SAT (average 1000, spread 200) aren't even on the same scale. You cannot compare 90 to 1200 directly — that's comparing apples to oranges.

The fix is the same trick from Part 2: measure both in standard deviations from average. Z = (x − μ) ÷ σ.

Do the math for both defaults below, and something clicks — they land at the exact same z. Same distance from typical, same level of "impressive," even though 90 and 1200 look nothing alike.

3.2 — Standardization
↳ A sneak peek at Day 2 — this exact trick has a name and a scikit-learn class.

Any bell curve → the same bell curve.

Run Z = (x − μ) ÷ σ on any normal distribution, and it always lands in the exact same place: average 0, spread 1. That specific shape has a name — the Standard Normal Distribution.

This is the exact mathematical engine behind StandardScaler in scikit-learn. Every time a column gets .fit_transform()'d, this is what's happening underneath.

3.3 — The Empirical Rule
↳ Now that we can measure distance from average in σ — how much of everything is actually nearby?

68, 95, 99.7.

Once you know a bell curve's average (μ) and spread (σ), you always know the same three numbers, no matter what the curve is measuring.

About 68% of everything sits within 1σ of average. About 95% sits within 2σ. About 99.7% — almost everything — sits within 3σ.

So if a kid's height is within 1σ of average, that's completely ordinary — 68 out of 100 kids are right there with them. Outside 3σ? That's maybe 3 kids in 1,000. Genuinely rare.

3.4 — Confidence Interval
↳ Here's what those three numbers are actually for.

Don't guess one number. Guess a range.

Someone asks: how tall will the next kid who walks in be? Guessing one exact number — "112cm" — is almost always wrong.

Instead, guess a range: "I'm pretty sure it's between 94cm and 126cm." That's a confidence interval — a window built around the average, sized using the spread.

How wide the window needs to be depends on how sure you want to be. That's exactly the 68/95/99.7 from the last slide, reused as confidence levels.

Bigger confidence needs a wider net. There's always a trade-off between being sure and being precise.
