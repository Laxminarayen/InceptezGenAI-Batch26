Every mistake has a name: residual

RESIDUAL
The gap between a real value and our guess. Residual = Actual (Y) − Predicted (Ŷ). Some employees sit ABOVE the mean line, some sit BELOW it.

Hour 2 · A sneaky problem

Why not just add up the mistakes?

Seems obvious: sum every residual to measure total error. Let's actually try it on our 42 employees.

Sum of raw residuals
—
Sum of SQUARED residuals
—
😬 Positive and negative residuals CANCEL each other out — the raw sum is basically zero, even though every single employee missed the guess! That's a useless error score.
✅ Squaring makes every mistake positive first — big misses count as big, and nothing cancels. This total is called the Sum of Squared Errors (SSE), and it's the honest way to score a guess.

Hour 3 · The Formal Model

Meet the classic straight line: Y = β₀ + β₁X + ε

Every line you just drew by hand already had a starting height and a tilt — you were eyeballing β₀ and β₁ the whole time. Let's give them proper names.

🎯

Y

Salary (₹ LPA) — the target we want to predict. The dependent variable.

💡

X

Years of Experience — the hint we use to guess. The independent variable / feature.

🚩

β₀ (Intercept)

Where the line starts when X = 0. The predicted starting salary for a fresh graduate.

📐

β₁ (Slope)

The weight — how much Y moves for every extra year of experience. The salary "raise per year."

🎲

ε (Epsilon)

The residual error — organic randomness (company, negotiation, role...) the line can't capture.

Notice: the Mean Line was secretly just this same formula with β₁ = 0 — a slope of nothing at all!

Hour 3 · Scoring a Line

Mean Squared Error: the quality score

MSE
MSE = (1/n) · Σ (Yᵢ − Ŷᵢ)²
Average the SQUARED residual across every employee. Smaller MSE = a line that hugs the data more closely.

Picture each residual as the SIDE of a square. Squaring the error is literally the AREA of that square — big misses create big squares fast.

Each shaded square's area = one employee's squared error. MSE is their average area. Remember this — in Hour 4, we stop averaging and start SUMMING these same squares. That sum has a name: SSE.

Hour 3 · How the Machine "Learns"

A game of hot and cold 🔥❄️

Exactly like the kids' game: someone hides an object, and you guess where — "warmer" means your last move helped, "colder" means it didn't. The machine plays the same game with its line:

1

Propose a handful of nearby lines

Nudge β₀ and β₁ by a small random amount, a few different ways.

2

Score every candidate

Compute the MSE for each nudged line — no guessing about quality, just measure it.

3

Keep the warmest, discard the rest

Lower MSE = warmer = keep it as the new "current line." Everything else is thrown away.

4

Shrink the nudges, repeat

Take smaller steps each round (cooling down) so it settles instead of overshooting forever.

Hour 4 · Building Toward R²

Square it, sum it, for all 42

Remember Hour 2: raw deviations cancel out, so we square them first. Do that to all three distances, for every single employee, and add them up. Three new names are born:

SST Total Sum of Squares

Σ(Yᵢ − Ȳ)²

What it explains: how much salaries vary overall — before X even enters the picture.

SSR Regression Sum of Squares

Σ(Ŷᵢ − Ȳ)²

What it explains: how much of that variation OUR LINE accounts for, using experience.

SSE Error Sum of Squares

Σ(Yᵢ − Ŷᵢ)²

What it explains: how much is STILL left over — the mistakes our line misses. (Same SSE as Hour 3 — just SSE = MSE × n.)

Hour 4 · Measuring Success

R²: the proportion explained

R² (COEFFICIENT OF DETERMINATION)
R² = SSR ⁄ SST  =  1 − (SSE ⁄ SST)
Both formulas give the SAME number. Since SST = SSR + SSE, "SSR's share of the total" is mathematically identical to "1 minus SSE's share."

Plug in the real numbers from the last slide and watch both formulas agree.

Hour 4 · The Two Extremes

R² = 0 vs. R² = 1

R² ≈ 0 — no relationship at all

The best sloped line is basically flat — it gives us NOTHING the Mean Line didn't already have. SSR ≈ 0, so SST ≈ SSE.

R² = 1 — a perfect fit

Every single point falls exactly on the line. SSE = 0, so SST = SSR — nothing left unexplained. (Rare in real life!)

Hour 4 · Why This Number Matters

Why data scientists love R²

📏

It's unitless

Salary in lakhs, dollars, or yen — R² always lands between 0 and 1. You can compare a salary model to a completely different weather model.

🗣️

It's instantly interpretable

"R² = 0.95" translates directly to English: "experience explains 95% of the variation in salary we see."

⚖️

It's the standard scoreboard

Every regression report, paper, and dashboard quotes R² first — it's the universal "how good is this model?" number.

⚠️

...but it has a blind spot

A high R² on data the model has ALREADY SEEN proves nothing about brand-new data. Coming up next: models that cheat.

🤔 Quick check before moving on: if SSE = 0, what is R²? If SSR = 0, what is R²? Look back at the formula — you already know both answers.

Hour 4 · The Evaluation Metrics Family

Meet the whole metrics family

R² isn't the only score in town. Here are the other measuring sticks you'll see everywhere — every one of them is built from the same residuals, just combined differently.

SSE

Σ(Yᵢ−Ŷᵢ)²

Sum of Squared Errors. The raw total — grows with more data. Mostly a building block (it's literally what OLS minimizes).

MSE

SSE ÷ n

Mean Squared Error. The AVERAGE squared miss — comparable across datasets of different sizes. Still in squared units.

RMSE

√MSE

Root Mean Squared Error. Back to ORIGINAL units (₹ LPA) — "on average, we're off by about this much." The most commonly reported metric.

MAE

(1/n)Σ|Yᵢ−Ŷᵢ|

Mean Absolute Error. Averages the raw (unsquared) distance — same units as Y, and less swayed by one huge miss.

Hour 4 · Pitfall 1 of 2

UNDERFITTING — too simple

UNDERFITTING
Forcing a rigid straight line through data that naturally bends — like years of experience vs. salary, which rises fast early on then plateaus later in a career (diminishing returns).

Watch the residuals: they're not random anymore. Junior AND very senior employees both sit ABOVE the line, while mid-career employees sit below — a systematic curve the line is blind to.

Years of experience vs. salary (₹ LPA). A straight line can't follow the curve's bend.

OVERFITTING — memorizing noise

OVERFITTING
A jagged, chaotic curve that hits every single training point perfectly — but has just memorized the noise, not the pattern.

Hour 4 · A Look Ahead

What if we had MORE than one X?

Real predictions rarely rely on just one feature. The same OLS idea scales up effortlessly — just add more β's:

Y = β₀ + β₁X₁ + β₂X₂ + β₃X₃ + ... + ε
🎓

X₁ — Education level

An advanced degree often shifts the whole salary curve upward.

🏙️

X₂ — City / cost of living

The same role pays differently in different cities.

🧑‍💻

X₃ — Role / skillset

Specialized skills command a premium on top of pure experience.

Same MSE. Same OLS logic. Just more dimensions — this is Multiple Linear Regression, our next stop.
