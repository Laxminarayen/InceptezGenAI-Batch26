Hour 1 · Naming the Problem

Meet the cost function

OPTIMIZATION
The general problem of finding the input that makes some function's output as small (or large) as possible. "Fitting a line" is secretly just optimization wearing a costume.
COST FUNCTION J(β₀, β₁)
J(β₀, β₁) = MSE = (1/n) · Σ (Yᵢ − β₀ − β₁Xᵢ)²
Feed it ANY pair (β₀, β₁) — any possible line — and it hands back ONE number: how bad that line is on our data. Our entire job today: find the (β₀, β₁) that makes this number smallest.

Notice: J takes the PARAMETERS as input, not X. We're no longer asking "what's the salary?" — we're asking "which line is best?" That's a totally different question, living in a different space.

Hour 1 · The Shape Isn't an Accident

Why is it always a bowl?

📊 Chart basics first: the x-axis (horizontal) is a number we get to pick — here, β₀. The y-axis (vertical) is whatever comes out of a function — a rule that takes one input and returns one output. Below, watch what comes out as we change what that rule does.
1

One employee's error is a straight LINE

Pick one employee. As β₀ slides left or right, their error (actual salary − predicted salary) changes at a steady rate — a steady rate of change is exactly what makes a graph a straight line.

2

Squaring bends the line into a BOWL

We square every error so positive and negative mistakes both count as "bad." Square a line and both ends flip upward — that single bend is a parabola: a mini bowl.

3

Add all 42 employees → still one bowl

Every employee adds their own parabola. Stack 42 of them and the total (MSE) is still one smooth valley. This special property is called convexity: no false valleys, no traps.

?

What if we used |error| instead of error²?

Absolute value also punishes negative and positive errors equally, and also has ONE minimum — but look closely: it forms a sharp V, not a smooth bowl. That corner has no well-defined slope, which breaks gradient descent (Hour 2). Squaring gives us the same "one minimum" guarantee and a smooth slope everywhere — that's why we square, not just take the absolute value.

👀 Hold onto this. Later courses (neural networks!) have BUMPY landscapes with many valleys. Simple linear regression is the well-behaved special case.

Hour 2 · Gradient Descent

The blindfolded hiker 🥾

🌫️

You're on a foggy hillside

You can't see the valley floor. You can only feel the ground right under your feet.

🦶

Feel which way is downhill

Tilt your foot. One direction feels steeper-down than the other — that's all the information you need.

👣

Take a step that way. Repeat.

Small step downhill, re-feel the slope, step again. Eventually you reach flat ground — the bottom.

This is the ENTIRE idea behind Gradient Descent — the algorithm that trains almost every machine learning model in existence. Let's do it for real, one parameter at a time.

Hour 2 · The Gradient Descent Algorithm

The actual algorithm, in five simple steps

1

Start with a guess — ANY guess

Playing a number-guessing game, your very first guess doesn't need to be good — you could shout "zero!" and still eventually win, because you'll get to correct yourself again and again. Same here: we just pick ANY starting numbers, even β₀ = 0 and β₁ = 0, and begin.

2

Feel the slope

Stand on a hill with your eyes closed. You can't see downhill, but you CAN feel it — rock your foot side to side, and one way tips down more than the other. That feeling, turned into a number, is the slope. One number tells you two things at once: its sign (+ or −) says which way is uphill, and its size says how steep it is. We compute one slope number for β₀ and a separate one for β₁ — two separate "feelings," one per dial.

3

Update: subtract the slope

new value = old value − (learning rate) × slope. Think of a thermostat: if it reads "+3" (too hot), you turn the dial DOWN. If it reads "−3" (too cold), you turn the dial UP. The slope behaves exactly the same way: a POSITIVE slope means "bigger is worse," so subtracting shrinks the number. A NEGATIVE slope means subtracting a negative — which is the SAME as adding — so the number automatically grows. One formula, both directions handled by the sign alone. The "learning rate" just decides whether we take baby steps or giant steps each time.

4

Repeat

Nobody reaches the bottom of a hill blindfolded in one giant leap — you creep down in lots of small steps, re-checking your footing after each one. So step 4 just means: go back to step 2 with your BRAND NEW numbers, and do the whole thing again. And again. And again.

5

Stop when the slope is ~0

How do you know when to stop taking steps? When the ground feels completely flat — no tilt at all, in any direction. That's a slope of (about) zero, and zero slope means "you're standing at the very bottom — there's nothing left to improve." That's your signal to stop.

THE TWO FORMULAS THAT MAKE STEP 2 WORK
slope for β₀ = −(2/n) × Σ (actual − predicted)
slope for β₁ = −(2/n) × Σ (actual − predicted) × (age)
Broken into plain words: "actual − predicted" is just ONE person's mistake — how far off was our guess for them? Add up EVERYONE's mistake and divide by how many people (n) — that's the average mistake. Multiply by −2, and that's the β₀ slope. (The 2 is just a fixed number left over from squaring each mistake back in Hour 1; the minus sign is exactly what makes step 3's subtraction always move us the right way.)
Tiny worked example — 3 people, actual salaries 4, 6, 5, and we guess β₀ = 3 for everyone: mistakes are 4−3=+1, 6−3=+3, 5−3=+2 → average mistake = +2 → slope = −2 × 2 = −4. Negative slope → rule says INCREASE β₀. Check: we guessed 3, but the real salaries are closer to 5 — of course we should guess higher next time! The formula and the common-sense answer agree.
The β₁ slope works exactly the same way, with one twist: each person's mistake gets multiplied by their age first, before averaging. That way, people with more years of age "count more" when deciding whether the raise-per-year dial needs to go up or down.

That's genuinely the whole algorithm. Let's see exactly where that formula comes from, then run it for real on a dataset.

Hour 2 · Where Do These Formulas Come From?

Deriving the slope formulas, step by step

STEP 1 — SET UP THE COST FUNCTION
J(β₀, β₁) = (1/n) Σᵢ (Yᵢ − β₀ − β₁Xᵢ)²
Let eᵢ = Yᵢ − β₀ − β₁Xᵢ  (the residual — actual minus predicted — for person i), so:   J = (1/n) Σᵢ eᵢ²
STEP 2 — ∂J/∂β₀ (HOLD β₁ FIXED, DIFFERENTIATE W.R.T. β₀)
∂J/∂β₀ = (1/n) Σᵢ 2eᵢ · (∂eᵢ/∂β₀)  [chain rule: d(eᵢ²)/dβ₀ = 2eᵢ × deᵢ/dβ₀]
∂eᵢ/∂β₀ = ∂/∂β₀ (Yᵢ − β₀ − β₁Xᵢ) = −1  [Yᵢ and β₁Xᵢ don't involve β₀; −β₀ differentiates to −1]
⟹ ∂J/∂β₀ = (1/n) Σᵢ 2eᵢ(−1) = −(2/n) Σᵢ (Yᵢ − β₀ − β₁Xᵢ)
STEP 3 — ∂J/∂β₁ (HOLD β₀ FIXED, DIFFERENTIATE W.R.T. β₁)
∂J/∂β₁ = (1/n) Σᵢ 2eᵢ · (∂eᵢ/∂β₁)  [same chain rule, now w.r.t. β₁]
∂eᵢ/∂β₁ = ∂/∂β₁ (Yᵢ − β₀ − β₁Xᵢ) = −Xᵢ  [Yᵢ and β₀ don't involve β₁; −β₁Xᵢ differentiates to −Xᵢ]
⟹ ∂J/∂β₁ = (1/n) Σᵢ 2eᵢ(−Xᵢ) = −(2/n) Σᵢ (Yᵢ − β₀ − β₁Xᵢ)Xᵢ
🧮 The ONLY calculus fact used here is the chain rule for a square: the derivative of (something)² is 2×(something)×(derivative of that something). Everything else is just "does this term contain β₀ (or β₁) or not?"

Plain-English check: nudging β₀ shifts EVERY prediction by the same amount (that's the −1), so its slope is −2×the average residual. Nudging β₁ shifts each prediction by an amount proportional to that person's age (that's the −Xᵢ), so its slope carries an extra Xᵢ — −2×the average of (residual×age).

Solid line = current guess. Dashed line = β₀ nudged up a little. Every dashed residual shrinks by the SAME amount — that's the "−1" from Step 2, drawn.

Hour 2 · The Two Dials Are Connected

Why can't we just find β₀, then find β₁?

predicted salary = β₀ + β₁ × age. There's only ONE mistake per person, and BOTH dials shape it together — you can't split a mistake into "the β₀ part" and "the β₁ part." Nudge either dial and the very same mistake numbers change. That means: the best β₀ depends on what β₁ currently is, and the best β₁ depends on what β₀ currently is — like a seesaw, move one side and the balance point on the other side shifts too.

Hour 2 · Naming the Feeling

The "slope under your feet" has a name: the gradient

GRADIENT
At any point, the gradient is the direction of steepest INCREASE — the way that feels most "uphill." It also tells you HOW steep: a big gradient means a steep slope, a near-zero gradient means you're on flat ground.
⬆️

Gradient points UPHILL

So to go downhill, we step in the OPPOSITE direction — that minus sign is the whole trick.

📏

Size = steepness

Far from the bottom, slopes are steep → big steps. Near the bottom, slopes flatten → tiny steps automatically.

🎯

Gradient = 0 means "you're home"

Flat ground in every direction is the mathematical signature of the minimum.

In 1D, the gradient is just the familiar derivative/slope. In 2D (β₀ AND β₁ together), the gradient becomes an ARROW — pointing uphill in the full landscape. That's next.

Hour 2 → Hour 3

"Follow the gradient."
But what IS it, exactly?

We've been treating the gradient arrow as magic. It isn't — it's two ordinary formulas, one for each parameter, and they connect directly to something you already know: the residuals. Hour 3: let's derive them.

Hour 3 · The Actual Formulas

Our target: two questions

RECALL: THE COST FUNCTION
J(β₀, β₁) = (1/n) · Σ (Yᵢ − β₀ − β₁Xᵢ)²

The gradient is just two numbers, answered one question each:

1️⃣

∂J/∂β₀

"If I nudge the INTERCEPT up a tiny bit, how does the total cost change?"

2️⃣

∂J/∂β₁

"If I nudge the SLOPE up a tiny bit, how does the total cost change?"

Answer both, and you have the full gradient arrow. No new math beyond what you've already used — just the chain rule, applied twice.

Hour 3 · Nudge and See

What does "nudge β₀" actually do?

Bump β₀ up by a tiny amount h. EVERY prediction shifts up by exactly h (the whole line lifts). So every residual (Yᵢ−Ŷᵢ) shrinks by h. Push a squared residual (Yᵢ−Ŷᵢ)² down by nudging Ŷᵢ up, and calculus says its rate of change is −2(Yᵢ−Ŷᵢ).

Do this for β₁ instead: bumping β₁ by h shifts each prediction by h·Xᵢ — bigger shift for bigger X. So its rate of change carries an extra Xᵢ: −2(Yᵢ−Ŷᵢ)·Xᵢ.

🤔 Notice both rates involve the residual. That's not a coincidence — the gradient is fundamentally built FROM the residuals you already know how to compute.

Nudging β₀ shifts the whole line vertically by h — watch every residual change by the same amount.

Hour 3 · The Reveal

Sum it over all n, and you get the gradient

∂J/∂β₀

= −(2/n) Σ (Yᵢ − Ŷᵢ)

Plain English: −2 × the AVERAGE residual. Are our guesses systematically too low or too high, on average?

∂J/∂β₁

= −(2/n) Σ (Yᵢ − Ŷᵢ) · Xᵢ

Plain English: −2 × the average of (residual × X). Does our error get systematically bigger as X grows?

If both are zero — average residual is zero, AND residuals don't correlate with X anymore — there's nothing left to improve. That's the minimum.

β₁ ≔ β₁ − α · ∂J/∂β₁

Hour 4 · The Honest Question

If there's a shortcut... why walk at all?

THE NORMAL EQUATION (CLOSED FORM)
For simple & multiple linear regression, there's an exact algebraic formula — β = (XᵀX)⁻¹XᵀY — that jumps STRAIGHT to the minimum in one shot. No walking required. This is exactly what "OLS" has been computing for us all along.
✅

Use the shortcut when you can

For plain linear regression, the closed form is faster AND exact. Real libraries (like scikit-learn) use it by default.

🚧

...but the shortcut doesn't always exist

Logistic regression, neural networks, and most of modern ML have NO algebraic shortcut — the matrix math simply doesn't solve cleanly.

🌍

Gradient descent works everywhere

It only needs a cost function and its gradient — it doesn't care how ugly the landscape gets. That universality is the entire reason we learn it here, on the one problem where we can also check its answer.

Hour 4 · A Look Ahead

Not every landscape is a bowl

Linear regression's cost is always convex — a single smooth valley. But once models get more complex (neural networks!), the landscape can turn bumpy: many dips, and gradient descent can get stuck in a shallow one instead of the deepest one.

🥣

Convex (today)

One valley. Wherever you start, gradient descent always finds THE minimum.

🏔️

Non-convex (later)

Many valleys. Gradient descent can get trapped in a "local minimum" that isn't the best one overall.

👀 This is exactly why simple & multiple linear regression are the perfect place to LEARN gradient descent — you can verify it against the exact answer. Later, on messier landscapes, you'll trust the same walking algorithm without that safety net.
