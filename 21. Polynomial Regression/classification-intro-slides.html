Today's Example

Will a student pass, based on hours studied?

20 students. For each one: how many hours they studied (x), and whether they passed the exam (y = 1) or failed (y = 0). Notice the y-axis only ever has two values — never 0.3, never 7. That's the whole difference from every dataset we've used so far.

A number for x, a BUCKET for y. Let's see if the tool we already know — linear regression — can handle that.

Let's Try What We Already Know

Can we just... use linear regression?

Treat "passed" as the number 1 and "failed" as the number 0, then fit a straight line exactly the way we always have. To turn the line's output into a decision, add one rule: predict PASS if the line's output is ≥ 0.5, otherwise predict FAIL.

A Second, Deeper Problem

It doesn't even stay between 0 and 1

Even with the original, well-behaved 20 students — no outlier — look what the SAME fitted line predicts once you step slightly outside the middle of the data. A "124% chance of passing" or a "−22% chance of passing" isn't just unusual — it's meaningless. A straight line has no idea it's supposed to stay inside a valid range. It just keeps going.

Time to Design Something Better

So what would we actually need?

🔒

Always between 0 and 1

Every output is a legitimate probability — no exceptions, no matter how extreme x gets.

🛡️

Resistant to outliers

One weird, far-away point shouldn't be able to swing the whole decision for everyone else.

🌊

Still smooth & trainable

Needs a shape gradient descent can still walk downhill on — same optimizer, new terrain.

Three requirements. One famous curve satisfies all three at once. Let's meet it.
Next Week's Star, Previewed

Meet the shape that solves all three: the S-curve

Same data. Same outlier toggle from before. This time, watch a second curve — an S-shape — sitting alongside the straight line. No formula yet, no fitting algorithm yet — that's next week. For now, just notice what it does differently.

Notice: the straight line swings wildly when the outlier appears. The S-curve barely reacts, and never leaves the valid 0–1 range either way.
