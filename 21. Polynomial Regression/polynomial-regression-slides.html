Hour 1 · A 60-Second Recap

Quick refresher: gradient descent, in five steps

Everything today is built on the exact same optimizer from last time — no shortcuts, no new algorithm. If any of these five steps feels unfamiliar, it's worth revisiting the Linear Regression & Gradient Descent deck first.

🎲

1. Start with a guess

Any starting numbers — even β₀ = 0, β₁ = 0 works fine.

🦶

2. Feel the slope

At the current guess, work out how the mistakes would change if you nudged each β.

➖

3. Subtract it

new β = old β − (learning rate) × slope — for every β, every round.

🔁

4. Repeat

Go back to step 2 with the new numbers. Over and over.

🏁

5. Stop when flat

Slope ≈ 0 in every direction means you're standing at the bottom.

🥣

Why it works

Every possible (β₀, β₁) scores a "badness" number. Plotted, those scores form one smooth bowl — walking downhill always finds its one true bottom.

👀 predicted salary = β₀ + β₁ × experience — a straight ruler, two dials. Today we're not changing the recipe above at all. We're changing what we hand it to work with.
Hour 1 · The Hidden Promise of a Ruler

A straight ruler makes
ONE unbreakable promise

Every extra year of experience is worth the EXACT same raise — whether it's your 1st year or your 21st. That's what "one constant slope, β₁" actually means. A ruler can tilt, and it can slide up or down. That's it. It can NEVER curve, speed up, slow down, or turn around.

Sometimes that promise is exactly true, and a straight line is perfect. But a lot of real life doesn't work that way — and when it doesn't, forcing a straight ruler onto it quietly produces a model that's confidently wrong. Let's go find some real-looking data where that promise breaks.

Hour 1 · Naming the Problem

The real drawbacks of linear regression

📏

Only ONE constant slope

It can tilt and slide — it physically cannot speed up, slow down, or turn.

🌊

Underfits curved reality

Diminishing returns, saturation, U-shapes, S-curves — all invisible to a straight ruler.

🚩

Residual patterns = a red flag

If leftover mistakes form a shape instead of random noise, the model is missing structure in the data.

🚀

Dangerous extrapolation

A straight line marches on forever. Predict "salary at 50 years experience" and it just keeps climbing, unrealistically.

⚖️

All-or-nothing effect size

It assumes every unit of X changes Y by the same fixed amount, everywhere — rarely true for real phenomena.

🎯

Outlier-sensitive

One extreme point can noticeably tilt the entire ruler (a lesson from Hour 4 of the last course, still true here).

🤔 None of this means linear regression is "bad" — plenty of real relationships genuinely ARE straight lines. The skill is knowing how to tell, and what to do when they're not.

Hour 2 · The "Aha" Moment

Polynomial regression is secretly still linear regression

THE FULL EQUATION
salary = β₀ + β₁·x + β₂·x² + β₃·x³ + ...
Yes, the CURVE this draws is bent. But look at how each β multiplies its own column and they all just get ADDED together — no β multiplies another β, no β is squared or divided. That's the actual definition of "linear": linear in the PARAMETERS, not linear in x.
🧩

x² is just another feature

To the optimizer, "experience-squared" is no different from "age" or "company size" was in multiple regression — just one more column.

⛰️

Same bowl, more dimensions

More β's means more axes on the cost landscape — but it's still one smooth bowl, still one true bottom.

🐾

Same gradient descent, unchanged

Compute each β's slope from the same mistakes, subtract, repeat. Nothing about the algorithm changes — only the number of columns.

✅ This is why we didn't need a new chapter of math — polynomial regression is a FEATURE ENGINEERING trick bolted onto the exact machinery you already know.
Hour 2 · The Exact Algorithm

Fitting the curve, gradient-descent style

Hour 1's five steps, unchanged — we're just handing gradient descent a longer list of columns to find slopes for. No closed-form shortcut anywhere in this deck: every curve you see from here on was walked downhill into place, one small step at a time.

1

Build the columns

x, x², x³, ... up to whatever degree you've chosen (Hour 2's earlier slide).

2

Normalize every column into [0,1]

x³ dwarfs x in raw size — normalize first, or the steps below go haywire. (Why, in detail, coming up next.)

3

Start with a guess

Every β — β₀, β₁, β₂, β₃, how many ever columns exist — starts at 0.

4

Compute ONE slope per β — same mistakes, every time

slope for β₀ = −(2/n)Σ(mistake)  |  slope for β₁ = −(2/n)Σ(mistake×x)  |  slope for β₂ = −(2/n)Σ(mistake×x²)  |  ...and so on, one extra term per column.

5

Update every β together, then repeat

new βₖ = old βₖ − (learning rate) × slope for βₖ — for k = 0, 1, 2, 3... all at once, every round, until every slope flattens to ~0.

🔁 Two β's or twelve β's — the loop body doesn't change shape, it just has more numbers riding along inside it. That's the whole reason this scales to any degree without inventing anything new.

Hour 3 · Two Ways to Get It Wrong

The student who barely studied vs. the student who memorized the answer key

😴

Underfitting (degree too low)

Barely studied. Gets the general idea but misses real patterns — high mistakes on the material they studied AND on the real exam. A straight line on curvy data: it's wrong basically everywhere.

📓

Overfitting (degree too high)

Memorized the practice test — including its typos and random quirks. Perfect score on THAT test, but the real exam is phrased differently, and they're lost. A wiggly degree-12 curve chasing every noisy dot: perfect on training data, terrible on anything new.

🎯 The goal isn't "as good as possible on the data we already have" — it's "as good as possible on data we HAVEN'T seen yet." That's the entire reason test sets exist.
Hour 3 · Why We Hide Some Data

The only honest way to catch overfitting

A memorizing student LOOKS perfect if you only ever quiz them on the exact practice test. The only way to catch the problem is a fresh question they've never seen. That's exactly what a test set is for a model.

1

Split BEFORE fitting anything

Set aside ~20% of the data. The model never sees it during training, at any degree.

2

Fit on training data only

Find the β's using just the 80% — same recipe as always.

3

Score on training AND test

Training cost tells you how well it memorized. Test cost tells you how well it actually LEARNED.

👀 A model that's great on training but bad on test isn't a good model that got unlucky — that gap IS the overfitting, measured directly.

Hour 3 · Naming What You Just Saw

It has a name: the bias-variance tradeoff

🎯

Bias — "consistently missing the same way"

A too-simple model (low degree) makes the SAME kind of mistake everywhere — like a dart player who always throws a little left, no matter how many darts they throw.

🎲

Variance — "wildly different every time"

A too-complex model (high degree) is EXTREMELY sensitive to exactly which training points it happened to see — a slightly different sample of employees would produce a shockingly different wiggly curve.

Low degree → high bias, low variance. High degree → low bias, high variance. The sweet spot from Hour 3's chart is the degree where their COMBINED damage to test performance is smallest.

Hour 4 · Nothing Is Free

Polynomial regression has drawbacks too

1

Extrapolation gets WORSE, not better

A high-degree curve doesn't just keep climbing past the data's edge — it can shoot off wildly in either direction. Watch the right edge of the chart →

2

Coefficients lose their plain meaning

"β₁ = one more year of experience" was easy to explain. "β₃ = one more (year)³" has no everyday intuition at all.

3

Choosing the degree is itself the hard part

There's no shortcut formula for "the right degree" — it takes exactly the train/test discipline from Hour 3, every time.

The shaded region is real training data. Past it, a high-degree curve is pure, often absurd, guesswork.

Hour 4 · A Look Ahead

What if we could keep
the extra columns, safely?

Instead of choosing a strict degree cutoff, what if the model could use LOTS of polynomial columns, but be actively discouraged from leaning on any single one too hard — automatically taming the wild wiggles? That's exactly what Ridge and Lasso regression do: the same cost function, plus a penalty for large β's. Not today's topic — but now you know exactly what problem they're built to solve.
