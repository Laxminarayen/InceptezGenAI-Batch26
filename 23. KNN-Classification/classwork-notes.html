K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN)

Every algorithm so far learned a fixed formula by minimizing a cost function. Today: one with no coefficients, no cost function, and technically no training phase — it just remembers every applicant it's seen, and asks "who are the K people most like you, and what happened to them?"

Hour 1 — Distance & Lazy Learning

Linear and Logistic Regression both fit a formula ahead of time by minimizing a cost function, then predict instantly forever after. KNN is a completely different family — called lazy learning (or instance-based learning): nothing gets learned ahead of time, all the real work happens the moment a new point shows up. One sentence, no math yet: "you are the average of the K people most like you" — the same idea behind a real-estate appraiser pricing a house from recent, similar sales.

Euclidean distance: d(A,B) = √[(x_A−x_B)² + (y_A−y_B)²] Pythagoras' theorem, reused. Worked example: credit 2→5 (Δ=3), age 25→29 (Δ=4) → d = √(9+16) = √25 = 5, a perfect 3-4-5 triangle. With more features, keep adding squared differences under the root — same formula, any number of dimensions. That's the only formula in the entire algorithm.

K=1 is fragile. If the single nearest neighbor happens to be an unusual point (e.g. a rejected applicant sitting in an otherwise-approved neighborhood), that one point can hijack the whole prediction. Bigger K is more stable against noise — but Hour 2 shows too big starts ignoring real local patterns. There's no free lunch, just a dial to tune.

Logistic Regression's decision boundary is always one straight line, because it comes from one linear formula. KNN's boundary has no such constraint — it bends, splits into islands, and wraps around outliers, shaped entirely by nearby data.

Hour 2 — The Full Algorithm & Choosing K

  1. "Train" the model — store every training point, exactly as-is. No iteration, no optimization, no convergence to wait for.
  2. A new point arrives — compute its distance to every single stored training point.
  3. Sort by distance — closest to farthest.
  4. Take the closest K — discard the rest.
  5. Majority vote — whichever label appears most often among those K wins.

Compare this to gradient descent's five steps (guess, feel the slope, subtract, repeat, stop when flat) — KNN has no equivalent of any of them. It's a genuinely different kind of algorithm, not a variation on the old one.

The single most important rule in KNN: scaling isn't optional. Age might range 18–70 (a span of 52); credit index might range 1–10 (a span of 9). In raw distance, a 1-year age gap and a 1-point credit gap aren't remotely comparable — age quietly dominates every calculation, and a worked hand-example can flip its own answer once features are standardized (z-scored) first. For Logistic Regression, skipping scaling just slowed gradient descent down. For KNN, skipping it gives an actively wrong neighbor set — a hard requirement, every time.

Euclidean (L2) distance is the default, but not the only option — Manhattan (L1) distance (Σ|xᵢ−yᵢ|, walking a city grid block by block) is less sensitive to outliers in any one dimension. Which one to use rarely matters as much as scaling correctly does, but it's a design decision, not a law of nature.

Picking K is a measurement, not a guess

Same discipline as choosing a polynomial degree: split into train/test once, try every reasonable K, fit on training only, score honestly on the held-out set.

  • K=1 — perfect training accuracy, trivially (a point's own nearest neighbor is always itself). Tells you nothing about new data.
  • A well-chosen middle K — training accuracy has settled, test accuracy is high, and no longer fooled by any single outlier.
  • Very large K — both curves sag; with K approaching the size of the whole training set, "majority vote" barely means anything local anymore.

Two practical refinements: ties (an even K can produce a tied vote — prefer odd K for 2-class problems, or fall back to the single nearest neighbor), and distance-weighted voting (let closer neighbors count for more than farther ones, instead of every one of the K votes counting equally).

"No training phase" just moves the cost to prediction time — every single prediction requires computing distance to every stored training point (n distance calculations per prediction, for n applicants). Linear/Logistic Regression pay a training cost once and predict instantly forever after; KNN pays nothing upfront and a little every single time.

Hour 3 — Evaluating Honestly

KNN has no training phase to overfit during — but a tiny K essentially memorizes each training point's own label, so the same discipline applies: split before doing anything, and critically, fit the scaler using only the training data's mean/std — computing it from the full dataset lets test information leak backward into training. On a dataset where the true "approve" region splits into two very different groups (young/high-credit and older/moderate-credit) around an unusual rejected outlier, Logistic Regression's single straight line genuinely can't bend around both — KNN, with no such constraint, wins the honest head-to-head comparison.

The curse of dimensionality. With 2 features, "nearest" is intuitive. Keep adding features (salary, credit score, loan amount, zip code…) and eventually every point ends up roughly equally far from every other point — "nearest neighbor" stops meaning much. This is the main reason KNN is usually applied to a modest, carefully-chosen feature set rather than thrown at hundreds of raw columns.

Hour 4 — Real Data & Multiclass

A missing value isn't just inconvenient for KNN — the distance formula is undefined the moment one coordinate is missing. The same six raw-data problems from Logistic Regression's Hour 4 (duplicates, wrong types, casing typos, impossible values, missing values, unencoded categories) all apply, but two are especially dangerous for a distance-based model:

  • Categorical distance — one-hot encoding Employment gives a clean side-effect: the distance between two different one-hot categories is always exactly √2, and between the same category, 0. Never encode categories as raw 1, 2, 3 for KNN — that would claim "Retired" is numerically closer to "Self-Employed" than to "Salaried," an invented ranking the data never justified.
  • Missing values — median imputation, for the same outlier-robustness reason as before, but now a genuine prerequisite for the math to run at all, not just good practice.

Steps 1–6 of the cleaning pipeline (duplicates → types/text → range validation → imputation → one-hot encoding → standardization) are identical to Logistic Regression's — the same raw loan file goes through the same cleaning either way. Only the final fitting step differs: "fit" for KNN just means storing the cleaned, scaled training set.

Multiclass is free Logistic Regression needed genuinely new machinery (One-vs-Rest or Softmax) to go from 2 classes to K classes. KNN's algorithm never mentioned "2" anywhere — majority vote among neighbors works identically whether there are 2 labels or 20. Split "Approve" into "Standard" and "Premium" approval, and nothing about the algorithm changes — just tally 3 labels instead of 2.

KNN Regression, previewed: the exact same five steps, but swap the majority vote for an average of the K neighbors' numeric target values. "Predict this house's price as the average of the 5 most similar recently-sold houses" is literally KNN Regression in plain English — same distance formula, same K, one word changed: vote → average.

The Takeaway

No formula was ever fit. No cost function was ever minimized. Every prediction all session came from one idea: find who's most similar, and ask what happened to them. Scaling is mandatory (not a suggestion), K is chosen by honest cross-validation (not a guess), and multiclass needed no new machinery at all — proof that "learn a formula" was never the only way to predict.
