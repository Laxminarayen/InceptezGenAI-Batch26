Hour 1 · A Completely Different Family

Parametric models learn a formula. KNN doesn't.

Look back at the last two algorithms you built, and notice what they have in common — then meet the family that breaks the pattern entirely.

📏

Linear Regression

ŷ = β₀+β₁x. Fit by minimizing MSE with gradient descent. A fixed formula, forever, once trained.

🌀

Logistic Regression

p = σ(β₀+β₁x). Fit by minimizing log loss with gradient descent. Same idea, different formula.

📍

K-Nearest Neighbors

No formula. No β's. No cost function. No gradient descent. It just remembers the data, and compares.

🧠 This family is called lazy learning (or instance-based learning) — nothing gets "learned" ahead of time. All the real work happens the moment a new applicant shows up, not before.

Hour 1 · The One Formula KNN Actually Needs

"Similar" means close — but close in what sense?

Two applicants are "similar" if the straight-line distance between their feature values is small — the same distance formula from geometry class, just applied to data.

Euclidean distance: d(A,B) = √[(x_A−x_B)² + (y_A−y_B)²]
Pythagoras' theorem, reused. With more than 2 features, just keep adding squared differences under the root — same formula, any number of dimensions.
1

Worked example

Applicant A: credit=2, age=25. New applicant: credit=5, age=29.

2

Take the differences

Δcredit = 5−2 = 3.   Δage = 29−25 = 4.

3

Square, sum, root

d = √(3² + 4²) = √(9+16) = √25 = 5. A perfect 3-4-5 triangle — distance = 5.

📐 That's it. That's the only formula in the entire algorithm — everything else is bookkeeping around this one calculation.

Hour 1 · The Problem With K = 1

One neighbor can be a fluke

Go back to the last slide, place your applicant right next to #26 — credit 7, age 55, REJECTED — even though every OTHER nearby point in that region got approved. With K=1, that single unusual point can hijack the entire prediction.

🎲

K=1: trust one opinion

Whatever that single closest applicant's outcome was, copy it exactly — noisy, easily fooled by one odd data point.

🗳️

K>1: take a vote

Ask several nearby applicants and go with the majority — one weird outlier gets outvoted by its more typical neighbors.

⚠️ This is the central tension of the whole hour: bigger K is more stable against noise, but (as Hour 2 will show) too big starts ignoring real local patterns too. There's no free lunch — just a dial to tune.
Hour 1 · A Shape You Haven't Seen Yet

Logistic regression draws a line. KNN draws... whatever it takes.

A logistic regression boundary is always one straight line (or plane), because it comes from one linear formula. A KNN boundary has no formula constraining its shape at all — it can bend, split into islands, wrap around outliers, anything the data actually looks like.

📏

Logistic regression

One straight decision line, everywhere. Simple, smooth, but can only ever be a line.

🧩

KNN

A boundary shaped entirely by nearby data — jagged with small K, smoother with large K, never forced to be straight.

👀 Hour 2 builds this boundary for real and lets you watch its shape change live as K changes.

Hour 2 · The Entire Algorithm

Five steps — and step 1 is the whole "training" phase

1

"Train" the model

Store every training point, exactly as-is. That's it — no iteration, no optimization, no convergence to wait for.

2

A new applicant arrives

Compute the distance from this new point to EVERY single stored training point.

3

Sort by distance

Rank every training point from closest to farthest.

4

Take the closest K

Keep only the K nearest — discard the rest.

5

Majority vote

Whichever label appears most often among those K wins. That's the prediction.

✨ Compare this to gradient descent's five steps from Linear/Logistic Regression — guess, feel the slope, subtract, repeat, stop when flat. KNN has no equivalent of ANY of those. It's a genuinely different kind of algorithm, not a variation on the old one.

Hour 2 · "Distance" Has More Than One Definition

Euclidean isn't the only option

📐

Euclidean (L2)

√Σ(xᵢ−yᵢ)² — straight-line "as the crow flies" distance. The default, used all session.

🏙️

Manhattan (L1)

Σ|xᵢ−yᵢ| — distance walking a city grid, block by block. Less sensitive to any single huge feature gap.

🔧

Minkowski

(Σ|xᵢ−yᵢ|ᵖ)^(1/p) — a dial: p=1 gives Manhattan, p=2 gives Euclidean. scikit-learn's default metric.

🧭 Which one to use rarely matters as much as scaling correctly does — but it's worth knowing this choice exists, and that "distance" is a design decision, not a law of nature.

Hour 2 · Not a Guess — a Measurement

Sweep K, measure accuracy, pick the best

Same discipline as choosing polynomial degree: split into train/test once, try every reasonable K, fit on training only, and score honestly on the held-out test applicants. Watch both curves as K grows.

1

K=1: perfect training accuracy

Trivial and expected — a point's own nearest neighbor is always itself. Tells you nothing about how it does on NEW applicants.

2

K=5: the sweet spot

Training accuracy has settled, test accuracy is high (85.7%) — and, as the last slide showed, no longer fooled by any single outlier like Applicant #26.

3

Very large K

Both curves sag — with K in the teens out of only 19 training points, "majority vote" barely means anything local anymore.

🔢 Rule of thumb: start near √n (here, √26≈5), prefer odd K for binary problems to avoid ties, then confirm with a real train/test sweep like the one on the right.

Hour 2 · Two Practical Refinements

What about ties? What about distance?

⚖️

Even K can tie

K=4 with a 2-2 split has no majority. Fixes: prefer odd K for 2-class problems, fall back to the single nearest neighbor, or pick randomly.

🎚️

Distance-weighted voting

Instead of every neighbor getting one equal vote, weight each vote by 1/distance — a neighbor right next door counts more than one at the edge of the K-circle.

⚙️

scikit-learn's default

KNeighborsClassifier(weights='uniform') by default — pass weights='distance' to turn on distance-weighting.

Hour 2 · Nothing Is Actually Free

"No training phase" just means
the cost moved to prediction time

Every single prediction requires computing the distance to EVERY stored training point — for n applicants, that's n distance calculations per prediction, every time, forever. Linear/Logistic Regression pay a training cost once and predict instantly after. KNN pays nothing upfront and a little every single time it's asked. (In production, structures like KD-Trees or Ball Trees speed this up — a detail for later, not required today.)

Hour 3 · Same Discipline, Same Reason

Honest evaluation still means held-out data

KNN has no training phase to overfit DURING — but it can still "memorize" in a different sense: a tiny K essentially memorizes each training point's own label. The fix is identical to every other model this course: score it on applicants it never got to peek at.

1

Split before doing anything

Set aside a portion of applicants the model never touches while choosing K.

2

Scale using ONLY the training data's mean/std

A subtle trap: computing the scaler from the full dataset lets test information leak backward into training.

3

Score honestly on the held-out set

That score — not the training score — is what you report and trust.

📊 With only 26 applicants, a 7-applicant test set is small — one single mistake swings accuracy by about 14 percentage points. Worth remembering on the next slide before reading too much into any one number.

Hour 3 · A Warning Worth Knowing

KNN's weakness: too many features

With 2 features, "nearest" is intuitive — points cluster naturally. Keep adding features (age, salary, credit score, loan amount, employment type, zip code...) and something strange happens: in very high dimensions, EVERY point ends up roughly equally far from every other point. "Nearest neighbor" stops meaning much.

📉 This is called the curse of dimensionality. It's the main reason KNN is usually applied to a modest, carefully-chosen set of features rather than thrown at hundreds of raw columns — a consideration Logistic Regression is comparatively less sensitive to.

Hour 4 · A Missing Value Hits KNN Differently

You can't compute distance to a blank

A missing credit score isn't just an inconvenience for KNN — the distance FORMULA itself is undefined the moment one coordinate is missing. Every one of the six raw-data problems from the Logistic Regression session (missing values, duplicates, wrong types, casing typos, impossible values) still applies here — but two of them are especially dangerous for a distance-based model.

🕳️

Missing values

d = √((x−NaN)²+...) is meaningless. Must impute BEFORE computing any distance — there's no "skip this feature" option mid-calculation.

📏

Unscaled features

As Hour 2 showed concretely: an un-normalized column doesn't just slow anything down — it silently dominates every distance and corrupts every prediction.

🧹 Same cleaning pipeline as before — median imputation, one-hot encoding, standardization — but now presented as a genuine PREREQUISITE for the math to even run, not just good practice.
Hour 4 · What Does "Distance" Mean Between Categories?

"Salaried" minus "Retired" = ?

Euclidean distance needs numbers. One-hot encode Employment into Is_Salaried / Is_SelfEmployed / Is_Retired — same fix as Logistic Regression — and something new falls out: the distance between two DIFFERENT one-hot categories is always exactly √2, and between the SAME category is always 0. Distance now has a clean, sensible meaning for categorical data too.

Hour 4 · Putting It Together

The preprocessing stack, now mandatory end to end

1

Remove exact duplicates

A duplicated applicant becomes their own nearest neighbor — an easy, silent way to inflate accuracy.

2

Fix types & inconsistent text

"29" → 29. "salaried" → "Salaried."

3

Validate ranges; flag impossible values as missing

A credit score of 910 doesn't just look wrong — it silently drags any distance calculation involving it far off.

4

Impute remaining missing values

Median, for the same outlier-robustness reason as before — required here, since distance to a blank is undefined.

5

One-hot encode categorical features

Gives every category a well-defined, equal-footing distance.

6

Standardize every numeric feature

Not a recommendation — a requirement for distance to mean anything at all.

7

Train/test split

Same discipline as every model this course.

8

Fit KNN

"Fit" here just means: store the cleaned, scaled training set. Prediction does the rest.

✅ Steps 1–6 are IDENTICAL to the Logistic Regression pipeline — the exact same raw loan file would go through the exact same cleaning. Only what happens in step 8 is different.
Hour 4 · The Thing KNN Doesn't Need Extra Machinery For

Multiclass isn't a special case — it's the same algorithm

Logistic Regression needed One-vs-Rest or Softmax — genuinely new machinery — to go from 2 classes to K classes. KNN's algorithm never mentioned "2" anywhere. Majority vote among neighbors works identically whether there are 2 labels or 20.

1

Same 26 applicants, finer-grained outcome

Reject (14) stays the same. The old "Approve" (12) splits into Standard Approval (8, older/moderate-credit) and Premium Approval (4, young/high-credit).

2

Nothing else changes

Same distance formula, same "closest K," same vote — just tally 3 labels instead of 2.

🎯 This is one of KNN's most underrated strengths: whatever works for binary classification already works for multiclass, with zero modification.

Hour 4 · Not Just For Classification

Swap the vote for an average,
and it predicts numbers instead

KNN Regression uses the exact same five steps — find the K nearest neighbors — but instead of a majority vote, it AVERAGES their numeric target values. "Predict this house's price as the average of the 5 most similar recently-sold houses" is literally KNN Regression, plain English. Same algorithm, same distance formula, same K, one word changed: vote → average.
