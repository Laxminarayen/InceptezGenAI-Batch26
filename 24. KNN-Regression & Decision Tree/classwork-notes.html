Hour 1 — KNN Regression

Last session's KNN classifier found the K nearest applicants and let them vote. Change exactly one word — vote → average — and you get KNN Regression: find the K nearest houses by size, and predict this house's price as the plain average of their sale prices. This is exactly how a real-estate "comp" report works: find a handful of the most similar recently-sold houses nearby, and average what they sold for.

Worked example: a new house of size 15 (hundreds of sq ft) — find its 5 nearest neighbors by size, then average their prices. That average is the prediction; no formula, no coefficients.

K controls the shape of the resulting prediction curve exactly the way it controlled KNN classification's decision boundary:

  • K=1 — chases every point; perfect on training data (a point's own nearest neighbor is itself) but jumpy and noisy on new houses.
  • A well-chosen middle K — test error (mean squared error — average of (actual − predicted)², lower is better) bottoms out here: smooth enough to ignore noise, local enough to track the real curve.
  • Large K — oversmoothed; too many far-away, dissimilar houses drag every average toward one flat number, and error climbs steadily.

KNN never actually "understands" anything about a feature — it only ever measures distance. Hour 2 introduces a family that works completely differently: one that looks at the data and figures out the smartest possible yes/no questions to ask, in the smartest possible order.

A Decision Tree's biggest edge: it's the only one of the three you can hand to a non-technical stakeholder and have them read the actual reasoning out loud — "if credit < 4.5, reject; otherwise, if age < 41…" No coefficients, no "K nearest neighbors" to explain. The tradeoff: deep trees overfit easily, and their boundaries are always blocky and axis-aligned — never smoothly curved like KNN's, never a clean straight line like Logistic Regression's.

A Decision Tree is, in one sentence, a pile of if/else statements. The shape was never the hard part — anyone can write an if/else. The only thing a computer has to figure out is: which feature, and which threshold, belongs in that "if"?

Entropy: a number for "how mixed up"

Picture two bags of 12 candies, red = rejected, green = approved. An all-one-color bag has zero surprise — "what will I pull out?" has an obvious answer. A 50/50 mixed bag is totally unpredictable. Entropy is a number for exactly this feeling: 0 means "totally sorted," and it peaks when a group is split as evenly as possible.

H(S) = −Σ pᵢ · log₂(pᵢ) For each label, take its share of the group, multiply by log₂ of that share, sum, and flip the sign. Worked on 26 loan applicants (12 approved, 14 rejected — nearly 50/50): H = −(0.46·log₂0.46 + 0.54·log₂0.54) ≈ 0.996, right near the maximum of 1.0 for two labels — exactly as mixed-up as two classes can be.

Building a real tree, by hand — Kaggle's Loan Prediction columns

A 16-row sample with mostly categorical columns (Married, Education, Self_Employed, Credit_History, Property_Area, Loan_Status) — the easiest place to practice entropy, since the groups already exist with no threshold to search for. A stump asks exactly one question and stops (no recursion); computing every column's stump reveals which one should go first.

Information Gain = H(parent) − weighted average of H(children) Credit_History alone gets 15 of 16 applicants to the correct decision from one single question — by far the best stump. It becomes the ROOT NODE: every applicant is asked this question first.

Splitting by Credit_History: the Credit_History = 0 branch is already pure (7 applicants, all REJECT, entropy 0) — that branch is finished forever. The Credit_History = 1 branch still has 9 applicants and isn't pure, so the entire stump exercise repeats on just those 9 rows (Credit_History is now constant there, so it can't help anymore).

Step Two (9 rows left): Property_Area wins (Information Gain 0.281 vs. Married's 0.197 and Education's 0.143), splitting into Semiurban (pure, all approve) and Urban (pure, all approve) leaves, plus a still-impure Rural branch of 2 applicants. Step Three (2 rows left, Rural): Married is the only column left that can still split anything — Information Gain 1.000, the maximum possible — and it perfectly separates the last two applicants. Every one of the 16 applicants now sits in a pure leaf. The tree is done: three levels deep at most, five leaves total.

What Information Gain really means

Divide Information Gain by the entropy at that node, and you get the fraction of that node's confusion the question eliminates — not just "bigger is better," a literal percentage. Credit_History alone wipes out 71.7% of all the uncertainty in the entire 16-row dataset; adding Property_Area brings cumulative resolved uncertainty to 87.5%; by the time the tree is finished, weighted entropy across all 16 applicants is exactly 0 — 100% resolved.

The same idea as R² in Linear Regression SST (spread around the mean, before fitting) ↔ H(root) (entropy before any split, spread around the 50/50 baseline mix). SSR (spread the line explains) ↔ Information Gain (confusion the split removes). SSE (spread left over) ↔ weighted entropy of the children (confusion still remaining). Both start from a baseline "spread" and measure what fraction one predictor accounts for — Linear Regression calls that fraction R², a Decision Tree calls it Information Gain.

Carrying Over to Next Class

Cut short on purpose right after finishing a complete hand-built tree. Three things carry over:

  • Numeric column splits — how a tree invents its own threshold for a column like age or income, the harder cousin of today's ready-made categorical groups.
  • The Gini Index — a different impurity formula, no logarithms required, and scikit-learn's default.
  • Decision Tree Regression — the same recursive splitting idea, aimed at predicting a number instead of a class.

The Takeaway

No formula was fit. No distance was measured. Every decision came from one repeated question: which column, split which way, removes the most confusion? — the exact same "explained fraction of a baseline spread" logic behind R², just built for categories instead of numbers.
