Hour 1 · A 10-Second Recap

Last time: find the K closest points, let them vote

That's the whole KNN classifier from last session, in one line. Today we keep every single part of that recipe — same distance idea, same "K", same "find who's nearby" — and change exactly one word.

KNN Classification (last time)

Find the K nearest applicants. Let them VOTE. Predict whichever label wins the most votes — "Approve" or "Reject."

KNN Regression (today)

Find the K nearest houses. AVERAGE their prices. Predict that average number. Same neighbors, different question asked of them.

Hour 1 · The Idea, With No Math At All

"Your house is worth what the 5 nearest houses sold for"

This is exactly how a real estate appraiser actually prices a home — a "comp" (comparable sale) report. They don't use a formula with coefficients. They find a handful of the MOST SIMILAR recently-sold houses nearby, and average what those sold for.

🏠

Real appraisal

"5 similar houses nearby sold for $180k, $195k, $205k, $210k, $220k → this one is probably worth about $202k."

📱

"People like you also paid..."

Predicting how much a new subscriber will spend, based on what similar past subscribers actually spent.

📏

KNN Regression, formally

ŷ = average of the K nearest neighbors' target values. No training, no formula fitting — just look, find, average.

Hour 1 · Not a Guess — a Measurement

Sweep K, measure error, pick the best

Same discipline as always: split into train/test, try every K, fit on training only, score honestly on held-out houses using mean squared error (MSE) — the average of (actual − predicted)², lower is better.

Past about K=15, error climbs steadily — too many far-away, dissimilar houses are dragging every average toward one flat number.

Hour 2 · What You Just Saw, Side By Side

Where Decision Trees actually win

PropertyLogistic RegressionKNNDecision Tree
Boundary shapeOne straight line / planeAny shape (smooth blobs)Any shape (rectangular blocks)
"Training" costGradient descent, iterativeNone — just store the dataRecursive splitting — fast, one pass
Prediction costInstant (one dot product)Compare to every stored pointWalk a handful of if/else checks — instant
InterpretabilityCoefficients (numeric weights)"Because these K neighbors" — hard to explainA literal, readable if/else path
Feature scalingRecommendedMandatoryNot required
MulticlassNeeds One-vs-Rest / SoftmaxFree — just count more labelsFree — just more leaf categories
🌳 Decision Trees' biggest edge: they're the ONLY one of the three you can hand to a non-technical stakeholder and have them read the actual reasoning out loud — "if credit < 4.5, reject; otherwise, if age < 41…" No coefficients to interpret, no "K nearest neighbors" hand-waving, no scaling step to get right first. Just a path anyone can follow.
⚠️ Nothing is free: that flexibility means deep trees overfit easily (you'll see this live with the depth slider shortly), and their boundaries are always blocky and axis-aligned — never smoothly curved like KNN's, never a clean straight line like Logistic Regression's.
Hour 2 · The Whole Algorithm, One Sentence

A Decision Tree is a pile of if / else statements

If you've ever written code, you already know the SHAPE of a Decision Tree. Every node is exactly this:

if credit_index < 4.5: predict REJECT else: # ask another if/else here, on the leftover group ...

The SHAPE was never the hard part — anyone can write an if/else. The hard part, the only part a computer actually has to figure out, is: which feature, and which threshold number, belong in that "if"? That's the whole rest of this hour.

Hour 2 · Before Any Formula: Just Look At Two Bags

How "mixed up" is a bag of candy?

Picture two bags of 12 candies each, red = rejected applicants, green = approved applicants. Which bag is more "confusing" to describe?

Bag A — all one color

Every candy is green. Zero surprise, zero confusion. "What color will I pull out?" has an obvious answer.

Bag B — a 50/50 mix

Totally unpredictable. "What color will I pull out?" is a genuine coin flip — maximum confusion.

🎯 Entropy is just a number for exactly this feeling: 0 means "totally sorted, no surprise" (Bag A). It's at its highest when a group is split as evenly as possible (Bag B). Nothing more mysterious than that.
Hour 2 · Turning "How Mixed?" Into a Number

The entropy formula — plain-English first

H(S) = −Σ pᵢ · log₂(pᵢ)
For each label, take "what fraction of the group has this label," multiply by the log₂ of that fraction, and add them all up (with a minus sign so the answer comes out positive). You will never compute this by hand in practice — scikit-learn does it — but here's what it means in plain terms: it rewards groups that are lopsided, and punishes groups that are evenly split.
1

Our 26 applicants: 12 approved, 14 rejected

p(approve) = 12/26 = 0.46.   p(reject) = 14/26 = 0.54. Nearly a 50/50 mix.

2

Plug into the formula

H = −(0.46·log₂0.46 + 0.54·log₂0.54) = 0.996

3

Read the number

0.996 is almost the maximum possible (1.0 for two labels) — exactly like Bag B. Makes sense: the applicants start out almost perfectly mixed.

Hour 2 · Entropy, Slowed All The Way Down

The full calculation — Yes column, No column, side by side

No shortcuts this time — every single arithmetic step, for BOTH classes, right next to each other.

Step"Yes" — Approved"No" — Rejected
1 · Count1214
2 · Proportion p = count ÷ 2612 ÷ 26 = 0.461514 ÷ 26 = 0.5385
3 · log₂(p)log₂(0.4615) = −1.1155log₂(0.5385) = −0.8931
4 · p × log₂(p)0.4615 × −1.1155 = −0.51480.5385 × −0.8931 = −0.4809
5

Add the two "Step 4" numbers together

−0.5148 + (−0.4809) = −0.9957

6

Flip the sign (that's the minus at the very front of the formula)

H(S) = −(−0.9957) = 0.9957 ≈ 0.996

🎯 H(S) ≈ 0.996 — right up near the maximum of 1.0. With 46% "Yes" and 54% "No," this group is about as mixed up as two classes can possibly be, and the number reflects exactly that.

Hour 2 · What Information Gain Really Means

IG is a percentage of the uncertainty you started with

Divide Information Gain by the entropy AT THAT NODE, and you get the fraction of that node's confusion a question eliminates. It's not just "bigger is better" — it's a literal percentage.

SplitEntropy before (H)Information Gain% of THIS node's uncertainty removed
Root: Credit_History1.0000.71771.7%
Step 2: Property_Area0.5030.28155.9%
Step 3: Married1.0001.000100.0%
🎯 One question (Credit_History) alone wipes out 71.7% of ALL the uncertainty in the entire dataset. That's why it's the root — no other single column comes close.

Hour 2 · A Parallel You Already Know

This is exactly what R² measures in Linear Regression

Recall Linear Regression: SST is the total spread of y around its MEAN, before any model. SSR is how much of that spread the fitted line explains. R² = SSR ÷ SST. Entropy and Information Gain are doing the identical job — just for a category instead of a number.

Linear RegressionDecision Tree
SST = Σ(y − ȳ)² — spread around the MEAN, before fitting anythingH(root) = entropy of Loan_Status before any split — spread around the 50/50 baseline mix
SSR = Σ(ŷ − ȳ)² — spread the fitted line explainsInformation Gain = H(root) − weighted H(children) — confusion the split removes
SSE = Σ(y − ŷ)² — spread left unexplained (residual)Weighted entropy of the children — confusion still remaining
R² = SSR ÷ SSTIG ÷ H(root)
Root split, "IG ÷ H(root)"
0.717 ÷ 1.000 = 0.717
Read exactly like R²
"71.7% explained"
🔁 Same underlying idea, both times: start from a baseline "spread" (the MEAN, for numbers — the overall class mix, for categories) and measure what fraction one predictor accounts for. Linear Regression calls that fraction R². A Decision Tree calls the identical idea Information Gain. Different formulas, same question: "how much of the total confusion did this explain?"
