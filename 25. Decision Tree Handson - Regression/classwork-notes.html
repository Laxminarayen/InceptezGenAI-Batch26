Numeric Splits — Inventing the Threshold

Every categorical column arrives pre-grouped — "Property_Area = Urban?" writes itself, the groups already exist. A column like ApplicantIncome has no such luck: it's just numbers, no ready-made buckets anywhere. The good news: the entropy math doesn't change at all. One new step gets bolted on — searching for a threshold.

  1. Sort by the column — all values, low to high.
  2. Take every gap between neighbors — the midpoint between two consecutive sorted values is a candidate threshold. 16 sorted values → 15 candidate gaps.
  3. Score every candidate exactly like a categorical split — split into "< threshold" / "≥ threshold," compute weighted entropy, compute Information Gain, keep the highest.

On the Kaggle Loan Prediction data's ApplicantIncome column, the best threshold (61.5) scores Information Gain 0.219 — enough to beat Married (0.106) and Property_Area (0.063), earning second place, but still not enough to unseat Credit_History (0.717) as root. The point isn't who wins — it's that a tree treats a numeric column as just another candidate in the exact same contest, once it does the extra threshold-search step.

The Gini Index — Entropy's Cheaper Cousin

Entropy needs a logarithm. The Gini Index measures the identical "how mixed up" feeling with plain multiplication instead.

Gini(S) = 1 − Σ pᵢ² Square each label's fraction, add the squares, subtract from 1. A pure group (p=1) gives Gini = 1 − 1² = 0. A 50/50 group gives Gini = 1 − (0.5²+0.5²) = 0.5 — its own "maximum confusion," just on a different scale than entropy's 0–1.

Re-scoring Credit_History with Gini gives weighted Gini 0.111 and Gini Gain 0.389 — a different number, same story: Credit_History massively reduces impurity either way. Run all five columns through both formulas and the ranking is identical, top to bottom — Credit_History wins, Married is #2, Property_Area is #3, Education and Self_Employed tie last with zero gain either way. Different numbers, same decisions: the normal case, not a coincidence. Both measures hit 0 at a pure group and peak at a 50/50 group; entropy peaks at 1.0, Gini peaks at 0.5, but the shape — and which splits look "good" — barely differs.

This is why scikit-learn's DecisionTreeClassifier() defaults to criterion='gini' — cheaper to compute, and picks essentially the same tree entropy would. criterion='entropy' is one keyword away if you want it.

Regression Trees — One Small Change, Again

Recall Hour 1 of the last session: KNN's one-word change from classification to regression was vote → average. The identical thing happens to Decision Trees. Instead of asking "which question un-mixes the classes the most?", a regression tree asks "which question makes the numbers inside each group most similar to each other?"

Variance replaces entropy/Gini as the impurity score "Spread out" is measured as variance — how far each value sits from its group's own mean, squared, averaged. A good split should make each new group's numbers land much closer together than the parent group's.

Worked on a Kaggle-style ApplicantIncome → LoanAmount dataset (32 applicants): the whole group starts at mean $193.3k with variance 3838.2; testing a split like "is income under 55?" and comparing the resulting groups' variances is exactly the same recursive procedure as the categorical case, just scored differently.

Every leaf in a regression tree is one flat, constant prediction — its group's mean — so a regression tree's prediction is always a staircase, never a smooth curve. More depth means more, narrower steps. Depth plays the same role here that K played for KNN:

  • Shallow (depth 1) — barely asks anything; one split for the whole dataset, high error on both train and test. Underfit.
  • A well-chosen middle depth — test error bottoms out; enough questions to track the real trend, not enough to chase noise.
  • Deep trees — training error shrinks toward 0 as depth grows, while test error climbs back up. Classic overfitting — a tree that's memorized rather than generalized.

The Takeaway

No formula was ever fit by a tree. No distance was ever measured. Just good questions, asked in the best order, over and over — for categories and for numbers, for splits that already exist (categorical) and splits a tree has to invent (numeric threshold search), for classification (entropy/Gini) and for regression (variance). Same recursive idea, four different flavors.
