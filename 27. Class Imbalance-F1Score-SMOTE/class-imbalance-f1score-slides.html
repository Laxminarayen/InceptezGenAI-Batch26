Confusion Matrix · Binary, Recap

Loan approval, one more time

Actual on the rows, Predicted on the columns (or vice versa — just stay consistent). The diagonal is always "got it right."

Accuracy = (True Approved + True Rejected) / Total
The simplest metric — and, as we're about to see, an easy one to be misled by.
Confusion Matrix · Precision & Recall, Plain English

Two questions, two different directions

P

Precision — "Of what I predicted, how much did I get right?"

When I predict Approved, how often is it actually Approved? Precision = True Approved / (True Approved + False Approved).

R

Recall — "Of what's actually true, how much did I catch?"

Out of all the real approvals, how many did the model actually find? Recall = True Approved / (True Approved + False Rejected).

Precision looks at your predictions; Recall looks at the actual world. They can disagree — that tension is exactly what F1 is built to summarize.
Confusion Matrix · Multiclass, Finished

Low / Medium / High — the full 3×3

Same idea, one row and one column per class. Off-diagonal cells are confusions between two specific classes.

Accuracy = Sum of the diagonal / Total
Generalizes cleanly. Precision and recall need one more step — pick a class at a time.
Confusion Matrix · Precision, Per Class

Same formula, one class at a time

ClassPrecision Formula
LowTrue Low / (True Low + confused-as-Low from Medium + confused-as-Low from High)
MediumTrue Medium / (True Medium + confused-as-Medium from Low + confused-as-Medium from High)
HighTrue High / (True High + confused-as-High from Low + confused-as-High from Medium)

Every "confused-as-X" term is just a specific off-diagonal cell in that class's column — everyone the model called X who wasn't actually X.

Confusion Matrix · Recall, Per Class

Flip the denominator: actual, not predicted

ClassRecall Formula
LowTrue Low / (Sum of everyone who is actually Low)
MediumTrue Medium / (Sum of everyone who is actually Medium)
HighTrue High / (Sum of everyone who is actually High)
🚩 A model can rack up a great-looking accuracy by mostly predicting the majority class well — and quietly recall almost nothing from the smaller classes. That's next.

Imbalanced Classes · The Real Question

Can I engineer the data
so the model actually learns the rare cases?

The way we quantify performance has to change (more on that soon) — but first, can we change the training data itself?

Imbalanced Classes · Two Levers

Undersample the majority, or oversample the minority

✂️

Undersampling

Shrink the majority class down toward the minority's size (e.g. 455 → 55 fraud-sized). Simple — but may throw away valuable information and reduce the model's understanding of the majority class.

➕

Oversampling

Grow the minority class up toward the majority's size instead. Random duplication risks overfitting to the exact same few examples repeated over and over.

Imbalanced Classes · Smarter Oversampling

SMOTE & ADASYN — synthesize, don't duplicate

1

Random Oversampling

Literally copy existing minority rows. Easy, but risks overfitting — the model just memorizes the same few points harder.

2

SMOTE — Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique

Find each minority point's nearest minority neighbors (KNN), then generate brand-new synthetic points along the line between them. New data, not copies.

3

ADASYN — Adaptive Synthetic Sampling

Same idea as SMOTE, but generates more synthetic points near the minority examples that are hardest to learn — the ones closest to the decision boundary or that look like outliers.

Imbalanced Classes · You Don't Always Need 50/50

The right ratio is a business choice

Resampling to a perfect 50/50 split isn't mandatory — 55/45, 60/40, or even 70/30 can be the right target depending on how costly each kind of mistake actually is.

F1 Score · Quantifying Performance Under Imbalance

Accuracy fails — it favors the skew

The 83%-accurate fraud model was proof: accuracy rewards agreeing with the majority class, which is exactly the wrong instinct under imbalance. Precision and recall don't have that blind spot — and F1 is how we fold both into one number.

F1 Score · Four Ways To Average Two Numbers

Precision and Recall disagree — now what?

➕

1 · Arithmetic Mean

(a + b) / 2 — treats both numbers as equally "sized."

📉

2 · Exponential Moving Average

Weighs recent values more — built for time series, not a single pair of scores.

⚖️

3 · Weighted Moving Average

A manual weight per value — useful, but requires deciding the weights yourself.

🔁

4 · Harmonic Mean

Divide the count of values by the sum of their reciprocals. The one F1 actually uses — and here's why.

F1 Score · Why Harmonic Mean

A classic trap: average speed

Drive 60 km out at 60 km/h, then the same 60 km back at 20 km/h. What's your average speed for the whole trip?

✗

Arithmetic mean says 40 km/h

(60 + 20) / 2 = 40 — but you spent far more time at the slow speed, so this overstates how fast the trip really was.

✓

The truth: 30 km/h

1 hour out (60÷60) + 3 hours back (60÷20) = 120 km in 4 hours = 30 km/h.

=

That's exactly the harmonic mean

H = 2ab / (a+b) = 2×60×20 / (60+20) = 30 km/h. It weighs by how much each rate actually contributed — not just its face value.

Precision and Recall have the same issue as those two speeds — averaging them naively can flatter a model that's brilliant at one and terrible at the other.
F1 Score · The Flagship Demo

Drag precision & recall — watch F1 punish the gap

F1 is the harmonic mean of Precision and Recall — it represents the model's "true rate," and it drops fast the moment either number is weak.

Arithmetic Mean
0.600
F1 (Harmonic Mean)
0.450
F1 = 2 · Precision · Recall / (Precision + Recall)
Notice: the arithmetic mean barely reacts to the weak side, but F1 drops hard the moment either Precision or Recall is poor — exactly the honesty accuracy was missing.
