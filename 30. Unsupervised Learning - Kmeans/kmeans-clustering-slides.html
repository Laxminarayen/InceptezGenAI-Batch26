Recap · Every Algorithm Before Today

Supervised ML: X and a target y

Regression, Classification, every ensemble we've covered — all of them were given X (features) and y (the target / label) during training. The entire job was to learn a mapping from X to y, then check the prediction against the real answer.

Supervised Learning
X₁, X₂, X₃, X₄ … → y (target / label). A "supervisor" — the true label — exists in the training data to check your work against.

How Do I Annotate This Data?

Two options — one doesn't scale

1

Manually sit and annotate

A human reads every row and assigns a segment by eye. Slow, subjective, and falls apart past a few hundred rows.

2

Use a grouping algorithm

Let an algorithm find the buckets automatically, from the numbers alone — no pre-existing labels required. This is Clustering.

Goal: no pre-existing labels exist, and the algorithm is obliged to create groupings / patterns from the data automatically — "natural groups of customers," discovered, not defined in advance.
Unsupervised Learning, Defined

Find the natural pattern, with no answer key

Clustering
Group data points so that points in the same group are similar to each other, and different from points in other groups — entirely from the structure of X, with no y to supervise it.

K-Means is the most common clustering algorithm: pick a number of groups, K, and let the algorithm find K centers that best explain the data's natural structure.

The Core Idea

Every point joins its nearest centroid

A centroid is an imaginary "center of mass" for a group — not a real data point, just the average position of everyone currently assigned to it. K-Means alternates between two questions: "which centroid is each point closest to?" and "given these members, where should the centroid actually sit?"

The Algorithm, Step by Step

Four steps, repeated until nothing moves

1

Choose K

Decide how many clusters to look for. (Section 4 covers how.)

2

Initialize K centroids randomly

Drop K random points into the feature space as starting centers.

3

Assign every point to its nearest centroid

Measured by Euclidean distance (Section 3) — whichever centroid is closest "wins" that point.

4

Update each centroid to the mean of its members, repeat

Recompute each centroid as the average position of everyone now assigned to it, then go back to step 3. Stop when centroids stop moving, or a maximum iteration count is reached.

Measuring "Closest" · Euclidean Distance

d = √( (x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)² )

The straight-line distance between two points. Drag the two points below (Income, Spending) and watch the distance update live — this is exactly what K-Means computes, for every point against every centroid, at every iteration.

Distance = 91.92
Smaller distance = more similar. K-Means assigns each point to whichever centroid minimizes this number.

(60.0, 17.5)

Income: A = (25+85)/2 = 55. Spending: A = (80+88)/2 = 84. Same arithmetic for B. The centroids physically moved toward their members — and because they moved, re-running the assignment step (back to Section 3, step 3) could reshuffle who belongs where. K-Means repeats this assign → update loop until nothing changes.

Convergence

Repeat until the centroids stop moving

Each full round (assign → update) is one iteration. Centroids typically swing a lot in iteration 1, then settle into smaller and smaller adjustments. K-Means has converged once an iteration produces zero reassignments — or, in practice, once max_iter is reached.

The One Hyperparameter That Matters Most

Nobody tells you K — you have to choose it

Unlike supervised learning, there's no accuracy score to tune K against — there's no ground truth. In practice: trial-and-error across a reasonable range (commonly K = 3 to 12), watching how much "error" drops as K grows. Drag K below and watch inertia — the sum of squared distances from every point to its own centroid — fall. This is the real Mall Customer dataset.

The "elbow" — where adding another K stops reducing inertia by much — is a common rule of thumb for picking K.

Rapid Fire

Six questions, answered

🏷️

Is there a "right" K?

No ground truth exists — K is chosen via the elbow method, domain knowledge, or trial and error.

🎲

Why random initialization?

Bad luck on starting centroids can land in a poor local optimum — scikit-learn re-runs with multiple random starts (n_init) and keeps the best.

📏

Does feature scale matter?

Yes, a lot — Euclidean distance is dominated by whichever feature has the largest numeric range, so features are typically scaled first.

🔤

What about categorical columns?

Euclidean distance doesn't make sense for categories — Hierarchical Clustering is often preferred when categorical features are present.

🚀

Can this be deployed?

Yes — exactly like Random Forest: joblib.dump the fitted model, then serve new predictions through Flask or Streamlit.

⚠️

Biggest limitation?

Assumes clusters are round and similar in size — real-world groups (e.g. elongated or nested shapes) can fool it badly.
