Recap · The Problem With Plain SGD

Each step trusts only the current gradient

wnew = wold − η·gt. The update depends only on gt, the current gradient — and different mini-batches give very different gradients for the same weight. The path zig-zags across the valley: gt is based only on the current value and is not intelligent.

🚩 Big, noisy gradients → the descent oscillates and wastes steps instead of heading straight for the minimum.
The fix we'll build: don't trust one noisy reading — average it with the recent past. That average is the Exponentially Weighted Moving Average (EWMA).
EWMA · Smoothing Noisy Ice-Cream Sales

More weight to the recent past, less to the old

A 3-day average treats all 3 days equally and forgets abruptly. An EWMA keeps a fraction of yesterday's estimate and blends in a fraction of today — a smooth, fading memory.

EMAt = 0.8·(previous EMA) + 0.2·(current value)80% of the old estimate, 20% of the new reading. Drag β below to change how long the memory lasts.
● raw daily sales  ·  — EWMA (smoothed)

Part II · Momentum

Apply the EWMA idea to the gradients

Replace the raw gradient gt with an EWMA of gradients, mt — a "velocity" that remembers which way we've consistently been heading.

mt = β·mt−1 + (1−β)·gtprevious EWMA + current gradient. Then step with the memory, not the raw reading:
wnew = wold − η·mt
Intelligent & previous-gradient-aware
Consistent directions reinforce each other and build speed; random zig-zags cancel out. The ball rolls through small bumps instead of rattling in them.
Momentum helps with capturing a consistent direction — smoothing the oscillation of plain SGD.
Momentum, Visually

Plain SGD rattles. Momentum glides.

On an elongated valley, SGD bounces wall-to-wall across the steep direction while barely moving along the gentle one. Momentum averages those opposing bounces to near-zero and keeps the steady forward component — so it heads down the valley far faster.

wnew = wold − η·mt  (gradient history)  vs.  wnew = wold − η·gt  (current gradient only)
Part III · RMSProp

What if one weight's gradients are huge and another's are tiny?

Momentum captures direction, but one fixed η still can't suit every parameter at once. RMSProp gives each parameter its own adaptive step by tracking an EWMA of squared gradients:

St = ρ·St−1 + (1−ρ)·gt²Squaring removes the sign and just keeps magnitude — "how big have this parameter's gradients been lately?"
wnew = wold − (η / √(St + ε))·gtε is a tiny nudge to avoid dividing by zero. RMSProp = Root Mean Square Propagation.
Self-correcting step: big recent gradients → large St → divide by a big number → shrink the step. Small gradients → grow the step. The learning rate adapts per parameter.
Two Ideas, Two Memories

Momentum smooths direction; RMSProp scales step size

🏂

Momentum — mt

EWMA of the signed gradients. Answers "which way have we consistently been going?" Builds velocity in a stable direction.

🎚️

RMSProp — St (or vt)

EWMA of the squared gradients. Answers "how big have the steps been?" Scales η down where gradients are large, up where small.

Both are just EWMAs — one of the gradient, one of its square. Combine them and you get the optimizer everyone reaches for first.
Part IV · Adam = Adaptive Moment Estimation

Momentum + RMSProp, together

Adam keeps both memories at once: mt (EWMA of gradients, the direction) and vt (EWMA of squared gradients, the per-parameter scale).

wnew = wold − (η / √(vt + ε))·mtMomentum supplies the numerator mt; RMSProp supplies the denominator √(vt+ε). Direction and step-size, handled together.
✅ Smoothed direction (momentum) + per-parameter adaptive step (RMSProp) = fast, stable, and forgiving of the initial learning rate.
The common default
Adam (and AdamW) is what you reach for first on most deep-learning problems. In PyTorch: torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=0.001).

Putting It to Work · MNIST Digits

784 → 128 → 10

Flatten each 28×28 greyscale image into 784 inputs → a hidden layer of 128 neurons (ReLU) → 10 outputs, one per digit. CrossEntropyLoss turns the 10 scores into class probabilities. Trained with Adam, lr = 0.001, mini-batches of 128.

model = nn.Sequential(
    nn.Flatten(),
    nn.Linear(28*28, 128),
    nn.ReLU(),
    nn.Linear(128, 10)
)
loss_fn = nn.CrossEntropyLoss()
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=0.001)

# 60,000 imgs / batch 128 ≈ 469 updates per epoch
for images, labels in train_loader:
    pred = model(images)
    loss = loss_fn(pred, labels)
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
