🏦 Hackathon · Binary Classification

Customer Campaign Response Prediction

NorthStar Bank — Customer Analytics Division

⏰ Deadline: 19 Sep 2026, 11:59 PM IST 📊 Metric: Macro F1 🔁 5 submissions / day 🧠 Logistic Regression · KNN · Decision Tree only

Objective

This isn't a textbook ML exercise — it's a simulation of the kind of assignment a Data Scientist receives in their first few months at a company. A business department has a problem; you are the Data Science team member who has to understand the requirement, turn it into an ML problem, prepare the data, build and evaluate models, and — most importantly — recommend something a non-technical stakeholder can act on.

The company

NorthStar Bank offers savings accounts, loans, credit products and investment products. One of those products is a Term Deposit. Right now, whenever Marketing launches a Term Deposit campaign, they phone every single customer in the database. That's expensive, it annoys customers who were never going to say yes, and it burns hours of call-centre time that could go toward customers who actually convert.

Your role

You are a Data Scientist on the Customer Analytics Team. Your manager has asked you to investigate whether historical customer and campaign data can predict who is Likely to Subscribe vs. Unlikely to Subscribe to the Term Deposit — so Marketing can prioritise their call list instead of dialling blind.

⚠ Watch for data leakage. The dataset includes a column called duration — the length of the phone call in seconds. Think about when the model actually has to make its prediction: before the call happens, to decide whether to make it at all. At that point, duration cannot possibly be known — it's a leak from the future. A big part of being a Data Scientist is asking "would this column actually be available at prediction time?", not just throwing every column at the model because it improves your score. We deliberately left duration in the raw data so you have to catch this yourself — a model that leans on it will look great on paper and be useless in production.

What your project should cover

Structure your notebook around these steps — this is exactly what a manager would expect to see in your writeup:

1. Understand the business problem

  • What problem is Marketing actually trying to solve, and why would an ML model help?
  • What exactly are you predicting? Is this regression or classification — binary or multiclass?
  • What would the bank do with your prediction once they have it?

2. Understand the data

Rows/columns, feature names, data types, numerical vs. categorical variables, missing/"unknown" values, duplicates, and the target distribution. What % subscribed vs. didn't? Is it balanced?

3. Exploratory Data Analysis

Investigate what actually matters to the business — subscription rate by age, job, education; the effect of housing/personal loans; previous campaign outcome; number of prior contacts; account balance. For every notable pattern, say what it could mean for Marketing — don't plot for the sake of plotting.

4. Data preparation

Separate features/target, encode categoricals, handle "unknown" values sensibly, drop columns that wouldn't be available at prediction time, split train/test, and scale numeric features — KNN is distance-based, so scaling isn't optional there.

5. Build three models

Logistic Regression, K-Nearest Neighbours (try a few values of K), and a Decision Tree (experiment with depth). You're expected to compare all three and recommend one.

6. Evaluate properly

Don't pick a winner on accuracy alone. Report Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1, and the Confusion Matrix for each model, and explain — in terms of this bank — what a False Positive means (you call someone who was never going to subscribe) vs. a False Negative (you skip someone who would have said yes). Which mistake should Marketing care about more?

Final business recommendation

Write this for the Head of Marketing, who is not a Data Scientist: which model, why, what it does well, where it fails, which customer segments look most promising, how the team should actually use it day-to-day, and what limitations they should know about before trusting it. "Decision Tree had the highest accuracy" is not a recommendation — explain why your pick makes sense for the business.

🎤 How to tell this story in an interview

A hiring manager doesn't want a walkthrough of your code — they want the shape of your thinking. Here's a scaffold you can adapt once you've actually built this:

"At NorthStar Bank, Marketing was cold-calling every customer for every Term Deposit campaign — expensive, and most calls went nowhere. I built a classifier to score customers as likely or unlikely to subscribe, using demographics and campaign history. One early finding mattered a lot: the raw data had a call-duration field that looked predictive, but it couldn't possibly be known before the call happens — so I dropped it to avoid a model that looks great in a notebook and fails in production. I compared Logistic Regression, KNN, and a Decision Tree, and evaluated them on [your Macro F1 / recall], not just accuracy, because only ~12% of customers actually subscribe — a model that says 'no' to everyone is 88% accurate and useless. I recommended [your model] because [your reason — e.g. best recall on the minority class, most interpretable for a non-technical stakeholder, most stable across CV folds]. In production, this would let Marketing call the top-N scored customers instead of the whole list." Fill in the brackets with your actual results once you've run the hackathon — that's the story that goes on your resume.

How this hackathon works

📤

Submission format

A CSV with exactly two columns: id,prediction. One row per id in test.csv, predictions must be exactly yes or no. See sample_submission.csv.

📊

Scoring metric

Macro F1 — the unweighted average of F1 across both classes. This rewards models that actually catch the minority "yes" class, not just ones that predict "no" for everyone (which would score ~88% accuracy for free).

🔒

Public vs. private

Your upload is scored instantly against a 2,712-row public fold — that's what you see on the leaderboard immediately. Final ranking is decided by the hidden 6,331-row private fold, revealed only after the deadline. This mirrors real Kaggle competitions and discourages overfitting to the public score.

🔁

Daily limit

5 submissions per day, resetting at midnight IST. Only your best public Macro F1 is kept — worse submissions don't hurt you.

⏰

Deadline

19 September 2026, 11:59 PM IST. Submissions are rejected after this instant — don't leave it to the last minute.

🧠

Models allowed

Logistic Regression, K-Nearest Neighbours, Decision Tree — that's it. No Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, Neural Networks, or AutoML. The point is demonstrating judgement with the tools you've actually learned, not chasing leaderboard points with a black box.

🏢 Hackathon · Multiclass Classification

Smart Building Occupancy Intelligence

NovaSpace Technologies — Enterprise Data Platform Team

⏰ Deadline: 19 Sep 2026, 11:59 PM IST 📊 Metric: Macro F1 🔁 5 submissions / day 🧠 Logistic Regression · KNN · Decision Tree only

Objective

This project puts you on the other side of the ML pipeline: you're a Data Engineer, not a Data Scientist. Your team ingests data from operational systems and makes reliable datasets available to the business — and now the Facilities team wants to know if a dataset your platform already collects can answer a new question. You have to judge the data quality first, then prove out a Proof of Concept classifier.

The company

NovaSpace Technologies runs multiple offices and has been investing in smart-building tech. Meeting rooms are wired with non-intrusive IoT sensors — temperature, light, sound, CO₂, and motion — streaming readings roughly every 30 seconds into the company's analytics platform.

The business problem

Facilities currently only knows whether a room was booked — not whether it's actually being used. Meetings get cancelled, an 8-person room might host 2 people, employees grab unbooked rooms, and lights/AC keep running in empty spaces. Facilities wants to know: can the existing sensor data estimate how many people (0, 1, 2, or 3) are actually in a room right now? This is a multiclass classification problem, and your job is to build the Proof of Concept — not the production system.

Think of the dataset as an enterprise data product

In a real company you wouldn't get a raw CSV straight off the sensors. Imagine a pipeline already happened upstream of you: IoT Sensors → Sensor Gateway → Raw Sensor Data → Enterprise Data Platform → Curated Room Sensor Table → your ML project. The data you're given represents that curated layer — but "curated" doesn't mean "ready for modelling." Inspect it carefully before you trust it.

What your project should cover

Part 1 — Understand the data

Rows, columns, feature names, dtypes, missing values, duplicates, the possible occupancy values, and how many rows belong to each class. What does one row actually represent?

Part 2 — Data quality assessment

As a Data Engineer, you don't jump straight to modelling. Are any readings missing? Duplicate timestamps? Unusual sensor values? Are all sensors on the same scale? Are the occupancy classes balanced? Are Date/Time useful predictors, or could timestamps leak unintended patterns into the model?

Part 3 — Understand the sensors

Does temperature rise with more occupants? Does CO₂ climb? Are occupied rooms louder? Does light level tell you anything? How strongly does PIR motion correlate with occupancy? Explain what each pattern means, don't just plot it.

Part 4 — Exploratory Data Analysis

How often is the room empty? Which occupancy level is most common? How do CO₂/sound/PIR distributions shift across classes? Are any sensors redundant with each other?

Part 5 — Prepare the data

⚠ Feature scaling matters here more than usual. Temperature sits around 20–30, CO₂ is in the hundreds, light intensity is on its own scale entirely. KNN calculates distance between points — an unscaled variable with big raw values will quietly dominate every distance calculation unless you standardise first.

Parts 6–9 — Build & evaluate three models

Logistic Regression (multiclass), KNN (justify your choice of K, don't pick it randomly), and a Decision Tree (compare a shallow tree, a deeper one, and an unrestricted one — does more depth always help on unseen data?). Report Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1 for each — and since this is multiclass, be explicit about how you're averaging them (this is exactly what "macro vs. weighted average" means, which you covered in Module 27).

Part 8 — Read the confusion matrix like a business person

Confusing 2 occupants with 3 is a minor miss. Confusing 0 occupants with 3 is a much bigger deal — Facilities might think a room is empty and switch off climate control while three people are actually in it. Find the model's worst mistakes and say why they matter.

Parts 10–11 — Translate the prediction back to the business

If the model predicts 0 occupants, what could Facilities do with that (energy savings, releasing an unused booking)? If it predicts 3, how might that be used differently (capacity planning, meeting overflow alerts)? Then sketch — at a high level, no need to actually build it — how this would flow at scale: Sensors → ingestion → cleaning → feature dataset → classification model → prediction table → Facilities dashboard.

Part 12 — Final recommendation, for the Head of Facilities (not an ML engineer)

Can occupancy be estimated at all? Which model do you recommend and why? How accurate is it, and what mistakes does it make? Which sensors matter most? And — important — would you deploy this across every office immediately? The source data came from one room, in a controlled window. Real offices have different sizes, sensor placements, and occupancy patterns. What would you collect before going from Proof of Concept to production?

🎤 How to tell this story in an interview

"At NovaSpace, Facilities only knew if a room was booked, not if it was actually used — meetings got cancelled, rooms sat empty with the AC running. I was asked, as a Data Engineer, to evaluate whether the sensor data our platform already ingested — temperature, CO₂, sound, light, motion — could estimate real-time occupancy as a 4-class problem: 0 to 3 people. Before I touched a model, I did a data-quality pass — checking whether Date/Time would leak information a live system wouldn't have, and whether the sensors were even on comparable scales, which mattered a lot for KNN specifically. I compared Logistic Regression, KNN, and a Decision Tree using macro-averaged F1, because the room is empty most of the time — a model that always guesses 'empty' would look accurate and be useless. I recommended [your model] because [your reason], and flagged that the biggest real-world risk wasn't the model's accuracy — it was that this Proof of Concept was trained on one room's sensor placement, and I wouldn't roll it out company-wide without collecting data from multiple room layouts first." Fill in the brackets with your actual results — this is the story that shows you think like an engineer, not just a modeler.
